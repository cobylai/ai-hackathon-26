The Lighthouse Keeper

The lighthouse stood at the end of a long stone breakwater, white against the grey Atlantic. Every evening the keeper climbed its one hundred and twelve steps with a can of oil in each hand, and every evening he paused at the gallery to watch the last fishing boats come home.

He had kept the light for thirty-one years. In that time the village had changed hands three times, the road had been paved twice, and the school had closed and reopened as a museum. The keeper noticed these things the way other people notice weather: without comment, but always with a quiet accounting.

Storm seasons were the hard part. When the wind came off the water at forty knots, the tower shook in a way that no amount of masonry could hide. The keeper would sit in the watch room with his logbook open, writing down the hour and the direction and the shape of every wave that broke over the rocks below. He believed the sea kept records too, and that one day the two sets of numbers would have to agree.

Young visitors often asked him whether he was lonely. He had a practiced answer, something about the gulls and the radio and the mail boat that came on Thursdays. The truth was less tidy. He was lonely in the way a tree is lonely, rooted in one place while the whole world moves past, and he had long since stopped treating that as a problem to be solved.

On the night the light failed, there was no storm. The lamp simply went dark at half past two, and the keeper, who had been dozing in his chair, woke to a silence that felt wrong. He climbed the steps faster than he had in years. The mantle was cracked. He had a spare, of course, wrapped in oilcloth in the store room, but replacing it took nineteen minutes, and in those nineteen minutes the sea was unmarked.

He wrote the failure up in the log the next morning, in his small even hand. Then he walked to the village and telephoned the service, and then he walked back and sat on the breakwater with his boots in the spray. The light had not failed in thirty-one years. He understood that this was the beginning of the end, and he found, to his surprise, that he did not mind.

For a while he kept a second logbook, a private one, in which he wrote down the ordinary things the official book had no room for: the colour of the water at dawn, the first tern of the season, the exact week the blackberries ripened on the leeward side of the tower. Nobody had asked him to keep it. He filled four hundred pages over eleven years and never read a single one back.

The village eventually put a plaque on the door, and the museum printed a small pamphlet with a photograph on the cover. In the photograph the keeper is standing at the foot of the steps with his hands in his pockets, squinting at the camera as though it had asked him a difficult question. Visitors stop there now and take pictures of the same spot, and sometimes a child asks who the man was, and someone reads the plaque aloud.

Two winters later the automation crew arrived. They installed a sealed lamp, a solar panel, and a small grey cabinet full of blinking lights, and then they shook his hand and drove away. The keeper stayed on for a month to show them nothing in particular. On his last evening he climbed the steps one final time, stood at the gallery, and watched the light come on by itself.
